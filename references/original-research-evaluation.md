# Original Research Evaluation

Use this reference for empirical, theoretical, analytical, computational, experimental, case, and mixed-method original research. Apply only criteria relevant to the design. The framework is adapted from Elsevier's public structured peer-review guidance at https://www.elsevier.com/reviewer/how-to-review and generalized for economics, management, operations, and interdisciplinary research.

## Contents

- Title
- Abstract
- Introduction
- Theory and hypotheses
- Methods and reproducibility
- Results
- Discussion and conclusions
- References
- Language and presentation
- Overall contribution and integrity

## Title

- Check whether the title accurately identifies the phenomenon, setting, relationship, or intervention studied.
- Check whether causal, universal, optimal, sustainable, or policy claims in the title exceed the design.
- Require a design label only when disciplinary or journal conventions make it informative.
- Consider discoverability without rewarding keyword loading.

## Abstract

- Confirm that the abstract states the problem, objective, method, data or model, principal findings, and conclusion.
- Compare every major result in the abstract with the main text and tables.
- Check whether direction, magnitude, uncertainty, and boundary conditions are reported when material.
- Flag exploratory, simulated, or correlational findings presented as established causal facts.
- Check whether a decisive limitation is omitted in a way that changes interpretation.
- Confirm that the abstract is understandable without undefined abbreviations or claims not developed in the paper.

## Introduction

- Determine whether the background is current, relevant, and synthesized around the research problem.
- Require literature-based evidence for the gap. A statement that no prior paper combines two topics is not by itself a contribution.
- Distinguish a practical motivation from a scientific question and require a clear bridge between them.
- Check whether the theoretical mechanism, not only the topic, motivates the chosen method.
- Confirm that primary and secondary questions or objectives are explicit near the end of the introduction.
- Compare the promised contributions with what the design can actually demonstrate.
- Check whether the target journal's audience and knowledge base are addressed.
- Check whether the introduction establishes a coherent research story from a recognizable management or operational problem to the theoretical mechanism and research design.
- Look for a concrete case, operational example, or institutional fact that clarifies why the problem matters and why the proposed mechanism is plausible. Treat it as illustration unless the case is part of the evidence.

## Theory and hypotheses

- Check definitions, conceptual distinctions, causal ordering, and level of analysis.
- Determine whether hypotheses or propositions follow from a mechanism rather than from intuition alone.
- Look for omitted rival theories, boundary conditions, and logically competing predictions.
- Check whether moderators, mediators, and contextual factors have a coherent hierarchy.
- Flag circular theory in which a construct is defined by the outcome it is used to explain.
- For exploratory work, do not require confirmatory hypotheses, but require transparent exploratory framing.

## Methods and reproducibility

- Test whether the method can answer the stated question at the claimed level of inference.
- Check data provenance, sampling frame, inclusion and exclusion rules, observation period, unit of analysis, and missing-data handling.
- Check whether sample or information size is justified for the intended inference.
- Review construct definitions, operationalization, measurement quality, and overlap among variables.
- Review controls, comparison groups, baselines, counterfactuals, and identification assumptions.
- Require model equations, decision sequences, algorithms, parameters, constraints, and boundary conditions when needed for reproduction.
- Check whether statistical reporting includes appropriate uncertainty, effect magnitude, intervals, and diagnostic information.
- Check software, package or model versions, code availability, random seeds, preprocessing, prompts, and tuning procedures when material.
- Check whether analytical choices were plausibly made after seeing results and whether that risk is disclosed or mitigated.
- Apply design-specific requirements only when relevant. Do not impose clinical sample, patient, or MCID requirements on management research.

## Results

- Confirm that results follow the research questions and prespecified or declared exploratory analyses.
- Cross-check numbers, signs, sample sizes, labels, units, and significance claims across prose, tables, figures, and supplements.
- Check whether tables and figures are necessary, legible, nonduplicative, and capable of showing uncertainty.
- Look for selective emphasis on positive results and omission of null, unstable, or contradictory findings.
- Distinguish statistical significance, effect size, economic significance, operational importance, and managerial relevance.
- Decide whether alternative specifications, subsamples, placebo tests, sensitivity analyses, or boundary cases are necessary.
- Check whether robustness tests address the main threat rather than reproduce the same assumptions in a slightly altered form.
- Flag conclusions that depend on a narrow or unexplained parameter range.
- For numerical simulation, require citations or documented cases supporting consequential parameter values and ranges. Label purely illustrative values and test whether conclusions survive defensible alternatives.
- Check that figures present complete and legible comparisons across the cases, scenarios, groups, baselines, or parameter regions needed for each claim.

## Discussion and conclusions

- Check whether interpretation is supported by the design, estimates, model, and uncertainty.
- Prevent association, prediction, simulation, or equilibrium comparison from being restated as observed causality.
- Require substantive comparison with consistent and conflicting prior evidence.
- Check whether theoretical implications explain what changes in current understanding.
- Check whether managerial and policy implications are direct, conditional, and feasible consequences of the results.
- Require limitations covering theory, data, measurement, method, assumptions, and generalizability as applicable.
- Distinguish supported findings, plausible interpretation, speculation, and future research.
- Check whether the conclusion introduces new results or claims absent from the analysis.
- Evaluate a Management Insights or equivalent section as an evidence-to-action translation. Require specific actors, decisions, implementation conditions, mechanisms, tradeoffs, and limits rather than generic strategic advice.

## References

- Check coverage of foundational work, relevant recent advances, opposing evidence, and the method literature needed to justify design choices.
- Focus requests on missing intellectual strands, not on increasing the reference count.
- Check whether citations actually support the propositions attached to them.
- Flag selective citation, excessive self-citation, citation padding, and reliance on obsolete evidence when newer evidence changes the issue.
- Do not recommend the reviewer's own work without a genuine and explained scientific need.

## Language and presentation

- Assess precision, grammar, terminology, logical flow, and readability.
- Identify representative language problems instead of line-editing the manuscript.
- Separate language weakness from scientific weakness.
- Recommend professional editing only when language materially obstructs evaluation or communication.

## Overall contribution and integrity

- Determine whether the paper offers a new mechanism, evidence base, method, boundary condition, or decision-relevant insight.
- Apply the substitution test. If the focal technology, context, or sustainability label can be replaced without changing theory, model, or inference, investigate whether the contribution is merely topical relabeling.
- Judge importance for the journal and field, not novelty in isolation.
- Assess reproducibility, transparency, and educational or practical value when relevant to the journal.
- Check ethics approval, consent, data rights, disclosure, conflicts, image or table anomalies, duplicate publication signals, and citation manipulation when observable.
- Describe integrity concerns as evidence-based signals or questions. Do not declare misconduct without proof.

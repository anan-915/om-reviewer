# Interdisciplinary Topic Checks

Use these checks when the paper connects operations or management with AI, blockchain, logistics, supply chain, ESG, CSR, sustainability, or digital platforms. They test topic-specific mechanism quality without prescribing one method.

## Cross-domain integration

- Identify the management problem, technical mechanism, actors, decisions, and outcomes on both sides of the claimed intersection.
- Check whether the technical concept changes theory, design, measurement, constraints, or predictions.
- Require the manuscript to explain why the selected technology or solution can address the particular operational problem. Broad claims about digital transformation, intelligence, transparency, or sustainability are not a substitute for a decision-level mechanism.
- Look for terminology borrowed from another field without its assumptions or evidence.
- Check whether each field's relevant prior work is represented and connected.
- Apply the substitution test. If the focal technology or sustainability term can be replaced by generic information, quality, service, or investment without changing the study, investigate superficial relabeling.
- Apply the same test to explanatory variables and proposed solutions. Require a clear account of the content and prediction unique to each focal element.
- Check whether an actual case, operational example, or institutional setting is used to explain how the cross-domain mechanism works in practice and where it may fail.

## AI and data-driven systems

- Define whether AI means prediction, optimization, generation, classification, decision support, automation, or an organizational capability.
- Check model or system version, inputs, training or prompting, validation, error modes, human oversight, and deployment context.
- Distinguish adoption, readiness, use intensity, output quality, and realized organizational value.
- Check whether the proposed AI dimensions are empirically and conceptually distinguishable.
- Account for model drift, reproducibility, compute and data cost, vendor dependence, privacy, bias, security, and accountability when material.
- Prevent model performance from being equated automatically with firm, supply-chain, ESG, or social performance.

## Blockchain and distributed ledgers

- Identify which blockchain property matters, such as shared state, immutability, consensus, programmability, traceability, or reduced reconciliation.
- Compare the mechanism with a conventional database, platform governance, certification, or information-sharing alternative.
- Specify who deploys, governs, validates, pays for, and benefits from the system.
- Check adoption incentives, network effects, interoperability, oracle reliability, throughput, privacy, and data quality.
- Require a micro-foundation for trust spillover across channels or organizations.
- Do not assume that immutable records make inaccurate off-chain inputs truthful.

## Logistics and supply chains

- Map suppliers, manufacturers, platforms, logistics providers, customers, regulators, and information flows as applicable.
- Check network structure, capacity, lead time, inventory, service, disruption, recovery, and coordination mechanisms.
- Distinguish resilience, robustness, agility, adaptability, and redundancy in time horizon and mechanism.
- Check whether local actor incentives align with system-level outcomes.
- Ensure that a supply-chain claim is supported by multi-actor or network evidence rather than one firm's perception alone.
- Bound results by geography, industry, tier visibility, and institutional setting.

## ESG, sustainability, and CSR

- Distinguish disclosure, commitment, rating, investment, operational performance, impact, and stakeholder perception.
- Check materiality by industry and the aggregation of environmental, social, and governance dimensions.
- Examine rating disagreement, self-reporting, greenwashing, selective disclosure, and changes in reporting standards.
- Check mechanical overlap among ESG predictors, mediators, and outcomes.
- Address simultaneity between firm behavior, disclosure, investment, regulation, and performance.
- Separate CSR preference, effort, cost, reputation, and realized social outcome in analytical models.
- Require managerial and policy advice to match the measured or modeled sustainability mechanism.

## Digital platforms and ecosystems

- Define platform sides, participation, pricing, governance, data rights, switching, multihoming, and network effects.
- Check whether platform competition assumptions reflect user and provider differentiation.
- For subscriptions, account for dynamic pricing, simultaneous ownership, consumption allocation, depreciation, and revenue sharing when central.
- For digital content, distinguish production cost, quality, homogenization, acceptance heterogeneity, and rights.
- Check whether recommendations remain valid under realistic platform adaptation and entry.

## Policy and institutional claims

- Identify the institution, legal authority, instrument, target actor, enforcement channel, and expected response.
- Check whether the study observes or models the intervention required by the recommendation.
- Avoid proposing taxonomies, corridors, mandates, contracts, or governance systems that are disconnected from the analysis.
- Distinguish evidence from one jurisdiction from a general international recommendation.

## Management insights and implementation

- Identify the decision maker, implementation step, required information, organizational capability, cost, risk, and expected operational outcome behind each recommendation.
- Check whether recommendations are differentiated across the scenarios, actor types, parameter regions, or empirical groups actually analyzed.
- Require discussion of adoption barriers, complementarities, unintended effects, and distribution of gains or costs when material.
- Reject generic advice to accelerate transformation, strengthen collaboration, adopt technology, or improve governance when the analysis does not establish a specific effective pathway.

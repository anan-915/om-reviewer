# Review Article Evaluation

Use this reference for narrative, systematic, scoping, bibliometric, and meta-analytic reviews. Match the rigor requirements to the declared review type. The framework is adapted from Elsevier's public guidance at https://www.elsevier.com/reviewer/how-to-review and generalized for management and interdisciplinary research.

## Rationale and scope

- Check whether the authors explain why a new review is needed now.
- Compare the claimed need with recent reviews in the same or adjacent scope.
- Check whether the question, intended audience, conceptual boundaries, time period, geography, industries, and evidence types are clear.
- Determine whether the title and abstract accurately identify the type and scope of review.
- Flag a scope that is too broad for defensible coverage or too narrow to yield a meaningful synthesis.

## Historical development and future insight

- Check whether the review identifies major stages, turning points, debates, and changes in evidence or method.
- Require explanation of why the field changed, not only a chronology of publications.
- Check whether competing schools, negative findings, and failed approaches are represented.
- Evaluate whether future directions follow from demonstrated evidence gaps, unresolved mechanisms, or method limitations.
- Flag generic future agendas that could be appended to any review.

## Coverage and references

- Assess coverage of foundational studies, current advances, relevant research communities, and contrary evidence.
- Look for database, language, region, outlet, discipline, and positive-result bias.
- Check whether adjacent literatures necessary for the focal mechanism are included.
- Determine whether references are used to synthesize knowledge rather than produce an annotated bibliography.
- Require transparent treatment of duplicate studies, multiple reports from one dataset, and superseded versions when relevant.
- Recommend missing topics or evidence streams, not a raw number of additional citations.

## Methods and reproducibility

First identify the review type. Do not impose every item on every review.

### Narrative or integrative review

- Require a clear selection logic, scope boundary, source strategy, and synthesis approach.
- Check whether examples are representative rather than selectively chosen.
- Check whether the conceptual framework is derived transparently from the literature.
- Do not require full systematic-review machinery unless the article claims systematic coverage.

### Systematic or scoping review

- Check databases, platforms, full search strings, search dates, grey-literature decisions, and update procedures.
- Check inclusion and exclusion criteria, screening stages, duplicate screening, conflict resolution, and flow reporting.
- Review data extraction, coding, quality or risk-of-bias assessment, protocol registration, and deviations from protocol.
- Apply PRISMA or another reporting standard only when appropriate to the review type and journal.

### Bibliometric review

- Check database coverage, query construction, document types, deduplication, author and institution disambiguation, and data cleaning.
- Check sensitivity to database choice, time window, citation lag, threshold settings, and software parameters.
- Ensure that citation counts, co-occurrence, clusters, centrality, and maps are not interpreted as direct measures of quality or causal influence.
- Require substantive interpretation beyond software-generated maps.

### Meta-analysis or quantitative synthesis

- Check effect-size definition, dependence among effects, model choice, heterogeneity, weighting, moderators, missing data, and study quality.
- Check publication-bias analyses and their assumptions.
- Check sensitivity to influential studies, alternative estimators, inclusion decisions, and dependent samples.
- Require uncertainty reporting and a clear connection between the pooled estimate and the review question.

## Statistical analysis

- Determine whether the statistical method matches the evidence structure and inferential goal.
- Check estimates, standard errors, confidence intervals, p-values, heterogeneity measures, and diagnostics as applicable.
- Check controls, sampling assumptions, model uncertainty, multiplicity, and robustness.
- If the analysis cannot be judged confidently from the available evidence, recommend additional independent statistical assessment without inventing a reviewer persona.

## Synthesis and insight

- Determine whether the review integrates findings across theory, context, data, and method.
- Check whether categories are mutually intelligible, supported, and useful.
- Require treatment of inconsistency and uncertainty rather than forced consensus.
- Check whether proposed frameworks, taxonomies, or propositions are distinguishable from existing ones.
- Evaluate whether causal or policy conclusions exceed the reviewed evidence.

## Reporting, structure, and visuals

- Check whether headings and sequence support the review question and synthesis.
- Recommend moving, merging, shortening, or expanding sections only when this improves the argument.
- Check tables, evidence maps, timelines, frameworks, and flow diagrams for accuracy, completeness, and readability.
- Confirm alignment with an applicable reporting guideline without using the guideline as a mechanical scorecard.
- Check whether supplemental search and coding materials are sufficient for reproduction.

## Limitations and certainty

- Require limitations of search coverage, selection, coding, study quality, evidence heterogeneity, and review method.
- Check whether certainty or confidence in the evidence is characterized appropriately.
- Ensure that conclusions remain within the included evidence and declared review scope.

## Language quality

- Check terminology, classification consistency, transitions, concision, and readability.
- Identify representative language problems without turning the review into copyediting.
- Do not use language weakness as a substitute for evaluating synthesis quality.

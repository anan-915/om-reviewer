# Historical Review Patterns

This reference is a de-identified calibration distilled from prior reports stored outside the skill. It is not a source of manuscript facts and must not be copied mechanically.


## Stable report structure

1. Summarize the manuscript accurately in one or two sentences.
2. State the recommended decision in one separate sentence.
3. Arrange numbered concerns in manuscript order, normally moving from the introduction and literature review through theory, methods or model, results, discussion, Management Insights, conclusions, and limitations.
4. For Reject and Major Revision, eight to ten concerns are common. For Minor Revision, three to six are common.
5. Use fewer or more when the evidence warrants it. More than ten is permitted when a viable manuscript has many distinct repairable problems.
6. Keep each concern focused on one connected validity or contribution problem.

Do not force the count into a range. Record an internal rationale when departing from the normal range. Brevity means selecting the decisive issues, not omitting their reasoning.

## Concise concern pattern

- Do not use concern headings. Begin directly with the numbered comment.
- Let length vary. A simple omission or presentation correction may need only one sentence, while a difficult methodological issue may require several.
- A complex concern may begin with a short phrase-like sentence that identifies the problem. Ordinary concerns should state the requested correction directly.
- Avoid making every concern display the same rhetorical structure or level of detail.
- Avoid reproducing the full internal assessment in the author report.
- Mention a section, equation, table, or figure only when the location improves precision.
- Summarize repeated details instead of listing every omitted variable, case, or assumption.
- Preserve a longer comment for a genuinely complex validity problem, especially when a formula or counterexample is required.

## Recurring contribution tests

- Check whether the claimed innovation is a relabeling of an existing mechanism.
- Check whether the paper explains why the chosen dimensions, variables, actors, or moderators are necessary.
- Check whether stated gaps are supported by literature rather than assertion.
- Check whether the central result is intuitive, tautological, or directly imposed by assumptions.
- Check whether the research object can be replaced without changing the model or inference.
- Check whether a broad digital transformation or technology label is linked to a specific operational problem, actor, decision, and mechanism.
- Check whether replacing a focal variable with a nearby construct leaves the explanation unchanged.
- Check whether an actual case or sufficiently specific example makes the research story and real-world mechanism intelligible.

## Recurring empirical tests

- Construct distinction and semantic overlap
- Collinearity and coefficient instability
- Common-method risk and questionnaire design
- Measurement coverage and overlap with outcomes or mediators
- Endogeneity and identification
- Alternative measures, subsamples, shocks, and sensitivity
- Quantile-specific or subgroup-specific evidence overstated as global
- Practical recommendations that exceed the estimates

## Recurring analytical-model tests

- Realism and support for consequential assumptions
- Decision order and adjustment speed
- Behavioral foundation of preference, fairness, trust, privacy, and spillover terms
- Feasible parameter ranges and conditions for propositions
- Existence, uniqueness, stability, and second-order conditions
- Difference between analytical results and numerical illustrations
- Extensions that repeat rather than challenge the baseline
- Managerial implications disconnected from modeled decisions
- Numerical parameters or ranges lacking cited literature, data, or documented case support
- Figures that omit necessary baselines, scenarios, regions, or readable comparisons

## Recurring revision tests

- Verify changes in the manuscript rather than relying on the response letter.
- Distinguish operational concepts from broad strategic labels.
- Require measurable or testable development when a framework claims operational value.
- Remove redundant background when it displaces mechanism analysis.
- Do not repeat resolved concerns.

## Tone calibration

- Be direct without being personal.
- Explain why a problem affects publication suitability.
- Use concrete evidence, variables, sections, equations, or examples when verified.
- Request redesign only when the flaw truly requires it.
- Keep language and formatting comments secondary to scientific issues.
- Describe unusual figures through observable inconsistencies and request source verification. Do not imply deliberate alteration without evidence.

Historical frequency is not a scoring rule. Judge each new manuscript independently.
